# How I Work — Self-Discovery Workbook

This file teaches your AI chief of staff how you actually work — your rhythms, habits, and preferences. It's the most important file in your system because almost every skill reads it.

**How to use this:** Read through each section. Answer the discovery questions honestly. Don't write what you aspire to — write how you actually work today. The examples show different patterns so you can see there's no single "right" answer.

---

## My Role

> **Why this matters:** Your AI needs to understand your scope and authority to give you appropriately-scoped advice and prep. A VP's meeting prep looks very different from an IC's.

**Think about:**
- What's your title and level? What decisions can you make without asking permission?
- How many people depend on you vs. how many people do you depend on?
- What's the ratio of "doing work" vs. "enabling others to do work" in your week?

**Example patterns:**

*Executive:* "I'm VP of Product. I make roadmap decisions, own P&L for our platform, and manage 3 directors who each have teams of 6-8. My job is 80% enabling others and 20% doing work myself. I have final say on feature prioritization but need to build consensus first."

*Manager:* "I'm an engineering manager with 5 direct reports. I split my time between people management (1:1s, career development, hiring) and technical leadership (architecture reviews, cross-team coordination). I can make technical decisions within my domain but escalate staffing and roadmap decisions."

*Individual contributor:* "I'm a senior data scientist. I own the recommendation engine and work with 3 product teams. Nobody reports to me but I influence a lot of decisions through my analyses. I'm the go-to person when someone needs data to support a case."

## My Daily Rhythm

> **Why this matters:** Your AI uses this to understand when to surface different kinds of information. If you do deep work in the mornings, it won't front-load meeting prep at 7am — it'll have it ready by the time you shift to meeting mode.

**Think about:**
- When do you start your day? When do you stop?
- When's your best thinking time? When are you just executing?
- What's the first thing you do when you open your laptop?
- How does your energy change throughout the day?
- Are there days that are structured differently? (Meeting-heavy Mondays, focus Fridays?)

**Example patterns:**

*Early bird, front-loaded:* "I'm up at 5:30, exercise, then deep work from 7-9 before meetings start. Meetings cluster 9am-1pm. Afternoons I'm lower energy — I use that time for emails, reviews, and admin. I try to be done by 5:30 for family dinner."

*Meeting warrior:* "My days are 80% meetings from 9-5 with almost no breaks. I prep on the commute in (train, 35 min). I do actual 'work' between 8-9pm after the kids are in bed. Fridays are slightly lighter — maybe only 4 meetings."

*Async-first:* "I work 10am-6pm with a long lunch break (walk + think). I batch meetings into Tuesday/Thursday and keep Mon/Wed/Fri meeting-free for focus. I process Slack in two batches — morning and late afternoon — not continuously."

### Your answer:

check Slack first thing but I do" stuff. Your AI needs the truth, not the ideal.]

## How I Capture Ideas and Notes

> **Why this matters:** Your AI needs to know where your unstructured thinking lives. If you dictate voice memos, it needs to know to check for those. If you scribble in a notebook, it can't access that (but it should know that gap exists).

**Think about:**
- Where do ideas land when they first hit you? (Voice memo, notes app, email to self, notebook, Slack DM to yourself?)
- Do you take notes during meetings? Where?
- How do you capture action items? (In the moment? After the meeting? Do they often fall through the cracks?)
- Is there a gap between where ideas land and where you actually look for them later?

**Example patterns:**

*Digital native:* "Everything goes into Notion. Meeting notes, random ideas, to-dos. I have a daily journal page that I type into throughout the day. I review it on Fridays."

*Voice capturer:* "I dictate voice memos constantly — in the car, walking between meetings, before bed. They're stream-of-consciousness and I rarely go back to listen to them, but they're important for getting thoughts out of my head."

*Paper-first:* "I use a physical notebook for meeting notes and a pocket notebook for random ideas. I transcribe the important stuff into my task manager at the end of each day, but honestly I only get to that 60% of the time."

*Minimal capturer:* "I don't really have a system. Things either stick in my head or they don't. I know this is a problem. I want the AI to help me fix this."

### Your answer:

```
[Describe how you actually capture information. Be honest about what works and what
doesn't. If you have a gap — ideas going into a void — name it. That's useful data.]
```

## My Meeting Patterns

> **Why this matters:** Meetings are where most knowledge workers spend the majority of their time. Your AI needs to know which meetings matter most, which ones you walk into cold, and which ones you have good systems for already.

**Think about:**
- How many meetings do you have in a typical week?
- Which recurring meetings matter most to your work?
- Which meetings do you consistently under-prepare for?
- Do you have a running doc or shared agenda for your 1:1s?
- What do you wish you remembered before each meeting?
- How do you handle meeting follow-ups? (Reliably? Inconsistently? Not at all?)

**Example patterns:**

*Over-scheduled exec:* "30+ meetings/week. My 1:1s with my team are the most important — I have shared docs for each one but rarely look at them before the meeting. Cross-functional meetings I just wing it. I'm usually context-switching so hard that I can't remember what I discussed with someone two days ago."

*Structured manager:* "15-20 meetings/week. I prep for my manager 1:1 carefully (15 min before). My team 1:1s I prep lightly. Standups need no prep. I take 5 minutes after each meeting to log action items in my task manager, which helps a lot."

*Meeting-light IC:* "Maybe 8 meetings/week. The important ones are cross-team syncs where I need to present data. I spend an hour prepping those. The rest are informational — I just show up and listen."

### Your answer:

```
[Describe your meeting patterns. Be specific about which ones you prepare for and
which ones you don't. The meetings you walk into cold are where an AI agent adds
the most value.]
```

## My Task and Project Management

> **Why this matters:** Your AI needs to know where your commitments live. If it generates action items, it needs to know where to put them. If it preps your week, it needs to see what's on your plate.

**Think about:**
- What's your main task management tool? (OmniFocus, Todoist, Things, Asana, Linear, a text file, your inbox, sticky notes, nothing?)
- Do you trust what's in it? Or is it incomplete and you rely on memory too?
- How do you plan your week? (Formal weekly review? Ad hoc? Just look at the calendar Monday morning?)
- How do you handle tasks that come in during meetings? (Capture immediately? Forget half of them? Write them in the wrong place?)
- Is there a gap between tasks being assigned and tasks being tracked?

**Example patterns:**

*GTD practitioner:* "OmniFocus with 15 projects and a weekly review on Fridays. I trust the system about 85% — some things slip through because I don't capture them fast enough during meetings. I use perspectives to focus: 'today,' 'this week,' 'waiting for.'"

*Calendar-driven:* "My task manager is my calendar. If it needs to happen, it goes on the calendar as a time block. I don't use a separate to-do app. This works for planned work but fails for ad hoc tasks and follow-ups."

*Minimal system:* "I keep a running list in Apple Notes called 'This Week.' I rewrite it every Monday morning. Things that don't get done either carry forward or get dropped. It's messy but it's honest about what's actually happening."

*No system:* "I'm going to be honest — I don't have a task management system. I rely on memory and my inbox. Things fall through the cracks regularly. I want to fix this but I don't know where to start."

### Your answer:

```
[Describe your actual task management approach. If it's a mess, say so. Your AI
can't help you improve a system it doesn't understand.]
```

- Do you have a system for processing messages, or do they pile up?

## What I Wish I Had More Time For

> **Why this matters:** This is the most strategic section. These are the problems your AI chief of staff should solve first. Start here when deciding which skills to build.

**Think about:**
- What tasks do you procrastinate on because they require prep work?
- Where do you feel like you're dropping the ball most often?
- What would you do with an extra hour each day?
- What used to work when your job was smaller that doesn't scale anymore?
- If you had a human chief of staff, what would you ask them to do first?

## Revisiting This File

This isn't a one-time exercise. Come back and update this file as your patterns change, as you discover new things about how you work, or as your role evolves. The more accurate this file is, the better your entire AI system works — because almost every skill starts by reading it.

A good cadence: revisit once a month, or whenever you feel like the AI is giving you advice that doesn't quite fit.
