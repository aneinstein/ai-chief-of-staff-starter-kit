# My Team — Relationship Map Workbook

This file teaches your AI about the people you work with — not just their names and titles, but how you actually interact with each person. Your meeting prep, 1:1 prep, and delegation skills all read this file.

**How to use this:** Start with the 5-7 people you interact with most. You can always add more later. The discovery questions help you think about each relationship in a way that's useful for AI prep.

---

## Before You Start: How Do You Relate to People at Work?

Different people have different working relationships. Before listing your people, think about what categories make sense for YOUR role.

**Example relationship types** (pick the ones that apply to you, or create your own):

- **Direct reports** — people you manage. You're responsible for their growth, performance, and removing blockers.
- **Your manager** — the person you report to. You're managing up — aligning on priorities, seeking guidance, building trust.
- **Peers** — people at your level in other functions. You're coordinating, negotiating, building alliances.
- **Stakeholders** — people who care about your output but you don't work with daily. Executives, cross-functional leads, internal customers.
- **External partners** — customers, vendors, consultants, advisors. The dynamic is different because they're outside your org.
- **Support roles** — EA, chief of staff, project manager. People who help you be effective.

You don't need to use these exact categories. The point is to have a vocabulary for the different types of relationships in your world.

## Your People

For each person, answer these questions. The discovery questions are there to help you think — you don't need to write answers to all of them. Just capture what feels useful.

### Person Template

Copy this block for each person:

```markdown
## [Name] — [Title/Role]

**Relationship type:** [from your list above]
**How often we meet:** [weekly 1:1, monthly review, ad hoc, daily standup, etc.]

**What I need to remember before meeting them:**
[What context helps you show up well? Recent projects they're working on?
Things you promised to follow up on? Their current stress points?]

**How we work together:**
[What does a good interaction look like? Are they direct or diplomatic?
Do they want to brainstorm or hear a recommendation? Do they need data
or narrative? Quick decisions or time to think?]

**Current themes:**
[What are the 2-3 things that keep coming up in your interactions right now?
These change over time — that's expected. Update them monthly.]

**Blind spots or friction:**
[Optional but powerful. Is there a pattern that trips you up? Do you
tend to under-communicate with this person? Over-promise? Avoid a topic?]
```

## Worked Examples

Here are three filled-in examples showing different relationship types. These are fictional people — use them as inspiration for level of detail, not as templates to copy.

### Example: A Direct Report

```markdown
## Morgan Chen — Senior Engineer

**Relationship type:** Direct report
**How often we meet:** Weekly 1:1, Tuesdays at 2pm

**What I need to remember before meeting them:**
Morgan is working on the API migration. They get frustrated when I don't remember
the details they shared last week, so I should review my notes before our 1:1.
They're also thinking about applying for the tech lead role — I should check in
on that periodically.

**How we work together:**
Morgan prefers written communication for technical decisions. They like to
bring a doc and walk through it. I should resist the urge to give answers
immediately — they process better when they can think it through. They
appreciate direct feedback but need it framed constructively.

**Current themes:**
- API migration timeline (on track but blocked by legacy auth system)
- Career growth — ready for tech lead scope, needs more visibility
- Burnout risk — working long hours, canceling 1:1s sometimes

**Blind spots or friction:**
I tend to jump to solutions when Morgan brings problems. They're usually
already 80% of the way to a solution and just need a sounding board.
I need to listen more and prescribe less.
```

### Example: Your Manager

```markdown
## Dana Park — VP of Product

**Relationship type:** My manager
**How often we meet:** Weekly 1:1, Thursdays at 10am; monthly strategy review

**What I need to remember before meeting them:**
Dana cares about outcomes, not process. Don't bring her problems without
a recommendation. She gets frustrated when she hears about surprises for
the first time in a group setting — flag things in our 1:1 first. She's
under a lot of pressure from the CEO on enterprise revenue.

**How we work together:**
Dana is direct. She'll say "I disagree" without softening it — that's not
personal, it's just her style. She respects pushback when it's backed by
data. She's great at seeing political landmines I miss. She prefers short
Slack messages over long emails.

**Current themes:**
- Enterprise pipeline — CEO is pushing hard, Dana needs me to deliver
- Reorg rumors — Dana hasn't said anything but I can tell she's stressed
- My promotion case — she said Q2, I need to remind her to submit it

**Blind spots or friction:**
I under-share with Dana. I wait until things are polished before bringing
them to her, and she'd rather see rough ideas early. I need to send more
"heads up" messages even when I don't have answers yet.
```

### Example: A Cross-Functional Peer

```markdown
## Riley Torres — Head of Customer Success

**Relationship type:** Peer / cross-functional partner
**How often we meet:** Biweekly sync, ad hoc when fires come up

**What I need to remember before meeting them:**
Riley cares deeply about churn. Any product decision that affects existing
customers lands on their desk. They're an ally when I keep them informed
and an adversary when they feel blindsided. Always give them a heads-up
before shipping changes that affect customer workflows.

**How we work together:**
Riley is consensus-oriented. They want alignment before decisions. I tend
to move fast and inform later — this has caused friction. We've gotten
better since I started sending a weekly "things coming down the pipe"
Slack message to their channel.

**Current themes:**
- Enterprise onboarding is too slow — this is a shared problem
- They want product to build self-service migration tooling
- Their team is understaffed and feeling it

**Blind spots or friction:**
I sometimes treat CS input as "customer complaints to manage" rather than
"market signal to learn from." Riley notices this and it erodes trust.
```

## Tips

- **Start with 5 people.** You can always add more. Start with whoever you meet with most frequently.
- **Update the "current themes" section regularly.** This is the most time-sensitive part. Once a month is a good cadence — or whenever you notice your AI's meeting prep feels off.
- **Be honest about friction.** This file is for your AI, not HR. The more honest you are about relationship dynamics, the better your prep will be. Nobody else sees this file unless you share it.
- **People change roles.** When someone moves, gets promoted, or leaves, update this file. Stale people data is worse than no data — it makes your AI confidently wrong.
